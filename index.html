What is Phishing?

Phishing is a cyberattack where criminals impersonate trusted entities to steal sensitive information — credentials, financial data, or personal details.

📧

Email Phishing

Mass fraudulent emails pretending to be banks, services, or colleagues to steal login credentials.

🎯

Spear Phishing

Highly targeted attacks using personal info gathered from social media to craft convincing messages.

🐋

Whaling

Attacks targeting high-level executives (CEOs, CFOs) to authorize large fraudulent transactions.

📱

Smishing / Vishing

Phishing via SMS (smishing) or voice calls (vishing) — increasingly common on mobile devices.

💡 Phishing accounts for 36% of all data breaches in the United States — it's the #1 attack vector.
📧

Recognizing Phishing Emails

Phishing emails have telltale signs. Learn to spot them before you click anything.

From: security@amaz0n-alerts.com ⚠️ Spoofed
To: valued.customer@email.com
Subject: ⚠️ URGENT ACTION REQUIRED!!! Your account is compromised ⚠️ Urgency

Dear Valued Customer ⚠️ Generic,

We have detected suspicious activity ⚠️ Vague threat on your account. You must verify your identity within 24 hours or your account will be permanently deleted ⚠️ False deadline.

Click below to verify your identity:

https://amazon.com/verify ⚠️ Fake link

Please also include your current password to confirm ownership. ⚠️ Password request

🚩 Red Flags

  • ❌ Spoofed or misspelled sender domain
  • ❌ Urgent, threatening language
  • ❌ Generic salutation ("Dear Customer")
  • ❌ Requests for passwords or credentials
  • ❌ Suspicious/mismatched links
  • ❌ Unexpected attachments (.exe, .zip)
  • ❌ Poor grammar & spelling errors
  • ✅ Hover over links before clicking!
💡 Always hover over a link to see where it actually leads before clicking. The displayed text can be completely different from the destination URL.

Spotting Fake Websites

Attackers create convincing fake websites to harvest your credentials. Here's how to spot them.

✅ Legitimate
🔒 https://www.paypal.com/signin
  • ✅ HTTPS with valid certificate
  • ✅ Correct domain spelling
  • ✅ Domain matches expected company
  • ✅ No extra subdomains or characters
❌ Phishing
⚠️ http://paypal.secure-login-verify.ru/signin
  • ❌ HTTP — no encryption
  • ❌ "paypal" is just a subdomain
  • ❌ Real domain is suspicious (.ru)
  • ❌ Contains "secure" to look trustworthy
🔍

Check the Address Bar

Look at the actual domain — the part between the last https:// and the first / after the domain name.

📜

Verify SSL Certificate

Click the padlock icon and check who issued the certificate. Phishing sites use free SSL certs too — HTTPS alone isn't enough!

🔎

Look for Typosquatting

Watch for: paypa1.com, goog1e.com, rnicrosoft.com (rn looks like m)

📋

Use Bookmarks

Bookmark important sites (bank, email) and only visit through bookmarks — never by clicking email links.

Social Engineering Tactics

Attackers exploit human psychology — not software vulnerabilities. Understanding these tactics is your best defense.

😱

Fear & Urgency

"Your account will be DELETED in 2 hours unless you verify now!"

Creates panic to bypass rational thinking. Attackers want you to act before you think.

🛡️ Take a breath. Real services give reasonable notice periods.
🏆

Greed & Reward

"You've won a $1,000 gift card! Claim it now — just verify your details."

Too-good-to-be-true offers designed to make you ignore warning signs.

🛡️ If you didn't enter a contest, you didn't win. Always too good to be true.
🤝

Authority

"This is the IT Department. We need your credentials to fix a security issue on your account."

Impersonating bosses, IT, government, or banks to gain compliance through authority bias.

🛡️ IT will NEVER ask for your password. Verify via official channels.
❤️

Familiarity

"Hi [Name], it's [Colleague]. I'm stuck — can you click this link and approve this invoice for me?"

Using names/info from LinkedIn and social media to seem like someone you know and trust.

🛡️ Verify unusual requests through a different channel (call the person directly).
🤗

Reciprocity

"We've already applied a $50 credit to your account! Just confirm your billing info to release it."

Offering something first to create a psychological obligation to give something back.

🛡️ Log in to your account directly to verify any credits or changes.
👥

Social Proof

"Over 10,000 users have already updated their security settings. Don't be left behind!"

Using herd mentality — if everyone else is doing it, it must be safe and legitimate.

🛡️ Legitimate notifications come from within the app, not from emails.
🔐

Best Practices & Defense Tips

Follow these proven strategies to protect yourself and your organization from phishing attacks.

🛡️ Email Security

1
Never click links in unsolicited emails

Navigate directly to websites by typing the URL or using bookmarks.

2
Verify sender's email address carefully

Check the full domain, not just the display name. paypal@paypal-secure.com ≠ paypal@paypal.com

3
When in doubt, call to verify

If an email from your "bank" or "IT dept" seems off, pick up the phone and verify using official numbers.

🔑 Account Protection

4
Enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

Even if attackers steal your password, MFA stops them from accessing your account. Use authenticator apps, not SMS when possible.

5
Use a Password Manager

Password managers auto-fill only on the correct domain — they'll never fill in credentials on a fake site.

6
Use unique passwords for every account

If one site is breached, credential stuffing attacks won't compromise your other accounts.

🏢 Organizational Practices

7
Report suspicious emails immediately

Use your email client's "Report Phishing" button or forward to your IT/security team. You might save a colleague.

8
Keep software and browsers updated

Updates patch security vulnerabilities that phishing sites and malware exploit to install themselves.

Real-World Examples

Famous Phishing Attacks

These real incidents cost billions. Learn from history.

2016
🎭

Google & Facebook Scam

Lithuanian man stole $122 million by impersonating a Taiwanese vendor via fake invoices...

Evaldas Rimasauskas registered a company with the same name as Quanta Computer, a real Taiwan-based manufacturer. He sent fraudulent invoices via email to Google and Facebook employees — who were accustomed to paying this vendor.

Both tech giants wired over $122 million before discovering the fraud. This is a perfect example of Business Email Compromise (BEC) — attackers don't need to hack systems when they can hack people.

Key Lessons:

  • Verify bank account changes through secondary channels
  • Implement approval workflows for large transfers
  • Train finance teams to recognize BEC attacks
2014
🏦

Sony Pictures Hack

A spear phishing email led to one of the most damaging corporate breaches in history...

Hackers (attributed to North Korea) sent targeted spear phishing emails to Sony Pictures employees. After gaining initial access via credential theft, attackers spent months inside Sony's network before unleashing destructive malware.

The breach leaked unreleased films, executive salaries, employee SSNs, and embarrassing internal emails. Estimated damage: over $100 million.

Key Lessons:

  • Network segmentation limits damage after compromise
  • Monitor for unusual data exfiltration patterns
  • Even large corporations are vulnerable to basic phishing
2020
🐦

Twitter Bitcoin Hack

A phone call convinced a Twitter employee to hand over admin access to 130 verified accounts...

Attackers called Twitter employees, impersonating the IT help desk. Using information gathered from LinkedIn, they convinced employees to provide credentials for internal admin tools. This is classic vishing (voice phishing).

The attackers took over 130 accounts including Obama, Biden, Apple, and Elon Musk, posting bitcoin scam tweets that netted over $120,000 in under an hour.

Key Lessons:

  • Never provide credentials over the phone
  • Implement zero-trust access controls
  • Verify caller identity through official channels before acting
2022
🔐

Twilio SMS Phishing

Fake SMS messages impersonating Twilio's IT department fooled employees into giving up credentials...

Employees received SMS messages claiming their Okta SSO passwords had expired, with a link to a convincing fake Twilio IT portal. The attackers had done reconnaissance to know employee names and use familiar-looking URLs.

The breach ultimately affected over 125 Twilio customers, including the Signal messaging app. This attack was part of a massive smishing campaign targeting dozens of tech companies simultaneously.

Key Lessons:

  • IT departments don't send password reset links via SMS
  • Use hardware security keys (FIDO2) — they resist phishing
  • Restrict employee phone numbers from public directories
